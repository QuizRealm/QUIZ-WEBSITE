The Archives of Quneitra & The Golan Heights

The southwestern plateau of Syria is defined by volcanic craters, commanding military elevations overlooking the Damascus basin, and ancient highland ruins. Explore the historical records below.

The Strategic Elevation Overlooking Damascus

The Quneitra governorate occupies one of the most fiercely contested and strategically vital geographical wedges in the Middle East. Rising steeply from the Jordan Rift Valley, the plateau forms an elevated volcanic barrier that commands a sweeping, unbroken line-of-sight across the entire Damascus basin to the east and down toward northern Israel and Jordan to the south and west. Throughout antiquity, any empire controlling these heights held absolute tactical superiority over southern Syria.

From Hellenistic generals and Roman legions to medieval Crusaders and modern armies, military commanders recognized that holding Quneitra meant controlling the gateway to the Syrian interior. The terrain is characterized by rugged volcanic cones (tells) such as Tel Abu Nida, which act natural observation posts. Lake Ram: The Volcanic Crater Lake

One of the most fascinating natural geological wonders in Quneitra is Lake Ram (*Birket Ram*), a deep, perfectly circular body of water nestled inside the caldera of an extinct volcanic cone.

Fed exclusively by underground subterranean springs and winter snowmelt rather than surface rivers, Lake Ram has inspired local folklore for millennia. Ancient legends whispered that the lake had no bottom, while modern hydrologists prize it as a pristine geological relic of the region's intense volcanic history.

Volcanic Soil & Highland Orchards

Like neighboring As-Suwayda, the Quneitra plateau is blanketed in thick layers of porous basaltic lava flows and rich volcanic ash. This specialized soil, combined with heavy winter precipitation and cool high-altitude summer temperatures, creates a unique microclimate.

The plateau is famous across Syria for producing exceptional highland agriculture, particularly sprawling orchards of sweet apples, cherries, and plums that thrive in the mineral-rich volcanic earth.

Ancient Ruins & Byzantine Monasteries

Beneath its modern geopolitical prominence, Quneitra is deeply layered with ancient history. Archaeological surveys across the ridge have uncovered Hellenistic farming estates, Roman milestone roads connecting Caesarea to Damascus, and early Byzantine monastic ruins carved from the basalt stone.

These ancient settlements demonstrate that the highlands were long prized not just for military defense, but as a prosperous agrarian and trading community linking the Mediterranean coast to the interior.

The Completion of the Syrian Archives

The city of Quneitra itself stands as a powerful historical monument. Heavily damaged during the mid-20th century conflicts, the abandoned old city was left standing as a striking ghost town adjacent to the modern administrative center, bearing witness to the turbulent modern history of the Levant. Yet, surrounding the ruins, the natural beauty of the volcanic plateau endures, with its green pastures, grazing cattle, and sparkling crater lakes.
