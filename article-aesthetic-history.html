Era No. 1

Earth & Harvest

70s

The Earth Tone Revolution

Following the psychedelic chaos of the 60s, the world grounded itself. The 1970s palette was defined by nature—but a very specific version of it. Harvest Gold, Avocado Green, and Burnt Orange dominated everything from fashion to kitchen appliances.

Why? The environmental movement was born, and the economy was stagnating. People sought comfort in warm, muddy, "organic" tones.

Memphis & Neon

The 1980s rejected nature entirely. This was the era of plastic, MTV, and artificiality. The palette shifted to high-contrast, electric hues: Cyan, Magenta, and Electric accents.

Why? The rise of digital technology and global optimism created a demand for colors that felt "new" and "synthetic." If it didn't look like a laser, nobody wanted it.

Era No. 3

Grunge & Corporate

90s

The Anti-Aesthetic

The 1990s had a split personality. On one hand, you had Grunge: flannels, deep crimsons, and dirty denims. On the other, you had early internet-era corporate minimalism: safe navies, grays, and clean whites.

Why? A rejection of the 80s excess. We wanted things to feel "authentic" and "raw" again, or clean and "user-friendly."

Y2K: Chrome & Cyber Blue

The late 90s and early 2000s imagined the future as glossy, translucent, and slightly alien. Think gel plastics, curved UI, metallic gradients, and optimistic tech branding. The palette leaned into Cyber Blues, Aqua, and Lilac—often paired with white and “chrome” neutrals.

Why? Consumer tech became personal. Interfaces moved into everyday life. Design started to look like software: shiny, rounded, and futuristic.

Era No. 5

Quiet Luxury

Now

Modern Minimal: Soft Neutrals

The current era leans toward calm: bone whites, warm grays, and near-black ink tones. It is minimalism with texture—linen, stone, matte surfaces—and a deliberate avoidance of visual noise.

Why? Screens are saturated. Brands compete in attention economies. The counter-move is design that feels quiet, premium, and breathable.
