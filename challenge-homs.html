The Archives of Homs & Palmyra

The Homs Governorate is a massive expanse bridging the lush western mountains to the deep eastern desert. Read below to understand the monumental history of the caravan kings, the warrior queen, and the crusader fortresses.

Queen Zenobia's Rebellion

No figure dominates the mythology and history of the Syrian Desert quite like Queen Zenobia (Bat Zabbai). Born in the 3rd century AD during a period of massive political instability known as the Crisis of the Third Century, Zenobia took power as the regent of Palmyra following the assassination of her husband, Odaenathus. Under her brilliant, aggressive leadership, Palmyra transformed from a wealthy but compliant Roman client state into a fiercely independent eastern empire.

Taking advantage of Rome's internal fracturing, Zenobia launched a stunning military campaign. Her heavily armored desert cavalry swept south, conquering Egypt, the breadbasket of the ancient world. She then pushed north, seizing vast swaths of Anatolia (modern Turkey). For a brief, shining moment, the Palmyrene Empire controlled the most vital trade routes and agricultural centers of the Mediterranean. She famously declared herself Empress, minting coins featuring her own face without the authorization of the Roman Emperor. Though Emperor Aurelian eventually defeated her forces in 272 AD, dragging her back to Rome in golden chains, her legacy as the ultimate warrior queen remains an indelible, romanticized pillar of Levantine identity.

The Wealth of the Caravans

Palmyra, locally known as Tadmur, did not generate its fabulous wealth from agriculture; it was entirely born of logistics. It sat perfectly in the arid expanse between the Roman Empire to the west and the Parthian (later Sassanid Persian) Empire to the east.

By securing the desert wells and offering armed escorts to massive merchant caravans crossing the treacherous steppe, Palmyra effectively taxed the entire flow of global luxury goods. Silk from China, spices from India, and pearls from the Arabian Gulf all passed through Palmyrene hands, generating the unbelievable capital required to build the monumental stone city in the middle of a barren wasteland.

The Great Colonnade

The architectural manifestation of Palmyra's wealth is best witnessed in the Great Colonnade. Unlike typical Roman streets, this monumental avenue stretched for over a kilometer and was not perfectly straight; it elegantly angled to connect the city's major temples and administrative buildings.

What makes the Colonnade unique is that halfway up each towering Corinthian column is a protruding stone bracket. These brackets were designed to hold bronze statues honoring the wealthy merchants, caravan leaders, and civic benefactors who funded the city's construction—a fascinating celebration of capitalistic enterprise over traditional imperial or religious dedication.

The Temple of Bel

The Temple of Bel was the religious epicenter of ancient Palmyra, dedicated to the Mesopotamian god of storms and fertility. Dedicated in 32 AD, its architecture is a masterclass in cultural synthesis, representing the melting pot of the desert crossroads.

While the exterior of the temple features classic Greco-Roman colonnades and monumental stone masonry, the interior spatial arrangement—specifically the inclusion of a flat roof for stargazing rituals and asymmetrical cult niches—is profoundly Semitic and Eastern. It stood as a physical testament to a society that operated seamlessly between two clashing superpowers.

Krak des Chevaliers

Moving far west from the desert sands of Palmyra, the Homs Governorate encompasses the rugged coastal mountain ranges. Perched atop a highly strategic 650-meter-high hill lies Krak des Chevaliers (Qal'at al-Hosn), universally acknowledged by military historians as the most perfectly preserved and formidable Crusader castle in the world. Originally the site of a small Kurdish fort, the location was given to the Knights Hospitaller in 1142, who spent the next century expanding it into an impenetrable military masterpiece.

The fortress is a textbook example of concentric castle design. It features two distinct, massive defensive walls separated by a steep ditch. If an invading army somehow breached the massive outer wall, they found themselves trapped in a kill-zone, exposed to devastating archer crossfire from the even higher inner walls. The castle was practically an independent city, featuring a massive Gothic chapel, soaring vaulted halls, granaries, and aqueducts. It successfully withstood numerous sieges by legendary commanders, including Saladin himself, before finally falling to the Mamluk Sultan Baibars in 1271 after a 36-day siege.
