The Archives of As-Suwayda & Jabal al-Druze

The volcanic highlands of southern Syria, dominated by Jabal al-Arab, contain Roman imperial cities, ancient sanctuaries, and a proud cultural legacy. Explore the historical records below.

Philippopolis (Shahba): City of an Emperor

One of the most extraordinary archaeological sites in As-Suwayda is Shahba, ancient Philippopolis. Founded in 244 AD by Roman Emperor Marcus Julius Philippus—commonly known in history as Philip the Arab—this planned imperial city was constructed in the exact birthplace of the Emperor. Unlike other Roman cities that grew organically over centuries, Philippopolis was laid out from scratch following a strict, classical grid pattern inspired by Rome itself, complete with a monumental theater, a sprawling public bath complex, and an imperial palace.

What makes Philippopolis visually striking is that every single building, column, and paved street was carved from the local dark volcanic black basalt stone, creating a dramatic aesthetic of imperial grandeur executed entirely in volcanic rock. Emperor Philip sought to immortalize his humble southern Syrian roots with a masterpiece of classical architecture. Jabal al-Arab / Jabal al-Druze

Geographically, the entire As-Suwayda governorate is defined by Jabal al-Arab (historically known as Jabal al-Druze)—a dramatic, uplifted volcanic massif featuring numerous extinct volcanic cones, rich basaltic soil, and high-altitude microclimates.

Rising abruptly above the southern Syrian steppe, these volcanic highlands catch moisture-bearing winds, creating cooler temperatures, winter snowfall, and fertile mountain slopes capable of sustaining extensive apple orchards, vineyards, and ancient winemaking traditions.

Kanawat (Canatha)

Nestled in the wooded foothills of the mountain massif lies Kanawat (ancient Canatha), a stunning Greco-Roman and Byzantine city that was once a prominent member of the Decapolis. Surrounded by oak forests and deep ravines, Kanawat features a magnificent theater, a colonnaded temple dedicated to the god Helios, and early Christian basilicas.

The city's black basalt architecture, integrated seamlessly with running water streams and ancient stone bridges, showcases an exceptional level of provincial Roman urban planning in the southern volcanic highlands.

Ancient Viticulture & Basalt Vineyards

The mineral-rich volcanic basalt soil of As-Suwayda has made it one of the premier winemaking regions in the Middle East since antiquity. Archaeological evidence across the governorate reveals hundreds of ancient rock-cut wine presses dating back to Nabataean and Roman times.

Today, local vintners continue this ancient tradition, harvesting grapes grown in volcanic ash that impart a distinct, mineral-rich complexity to regional wines, preserving an uninterrupted agricultural heritage.

The Volcanic Legacy of Southern Syria

The geological architecture of As-Suwayda is a testament to ancient tectonic and volcanic activity that blanketed the southern Syrian steppe in layers of dark basalt lava. This rugged terrain created natural defensive strongholds that have sheltered independent communities for millennia. From the Nabataean caravan guards and Roman emperors to the fierce autonomy of the Druze mountaineers in modern history, the inhabitants of Jabal al-Arab have always embodied the rugged, unyielding strength of the volcanic stone upon which they built their homes.
