There is a fundamental flaw in western relationship psychology when applied to the South Asian diaspora: it assumes the relationship is an isolated island of two people. In the Desi context—whether you are in Mumbai, Lahore, London, or New York—a relationship is never just two people. It is a highly complex socio-economic merger of entire extended families.

When you download standard "100 Questions to Ask Your Partner" lists from generic lifestyle magazines, they ask questions like, "What is your love language?" or "Where do you see yourself in five years?" While valid, these questions fail to address the actual pressure points that fracture South Asian marriages.

Module 1: The Boundary Protocol (Family & In-Laws)

The concept of a "nuclear family boundary" is relatively new in South Asian culture. Historically, loyalty to the joint family superseded loyalty to the spouse. This module tests a partner's ability to prioritize the marriage without disrespecting the parents.

Question 01
"If your mother heavily criticizes my career choices or parenting style, exactly how will you address it with her?"
Why you must ask this: A response of "I'll just tell her to stop" is insufficient. A response of "Just ignore her, that's how she is" is a massive red flag. You are looking for a partner who can articulate a specific, respectful, but firm boundary-setting strategy.
Question 02
"Do you expect us to live with your parents immediately after marriage? If yes, what is the timeline for us getting our own space?"
Why you must ask this: Ambiguity here breeds resentment. If living in a joint family is non-negotiable for them, you need to know immediately. If they promise "we will move out eventually," you need a concrete financial timeline, not a vague promise.
Question 03
"How much influence do your parents have over your major life decisions (career changes, buying a house, where we go on vacation)?"
Why you must ask this: This identifies the "Mama's Boy" or the "Enmeshed Daughter" syndrome. Seeking advice is a green flag; requiring parental permission to operate as an adult is a critical red flag.

THE "LOG KYA KAHENGE" SYNDROME

Many Desi partners will agree to boundaries in private, but abandon them in public due to the fear of "Log Kya Kahenge" (What will people say). Pay close attention to whether your partner modifies their behavior or abandons you to appease the extended community during weddings or family gatherings. Module 2: Financial Alignment & The "Black Tax"

Money is the silent killer of relationships. In the Desi diaspora, finances are complicated by the "Black Tax" or "Brown Tax"—the unspoken cultural expectation that adult children must financially support their parents, siblings, or extended family members indefinitely.

Question 14
"Are you currently financially supporting any family members, and do you expect to continue doing so after we are married?"
Why you must ask this: Sending money to retired parents is noble and common. However, hiding this financial outflow from a future spouse is financial infidelity. Both partners must agree on the percentage of household income allocated to extended family.
Question 15
"What are your expectations regarding my income? Will we have joint accounts, separate accounts, or a hybrid model?"
Why you must ask this: Traditional patriarchal norms dictate that the man provides while the woman's income is "hers." Modern economic realities often require dual incomes. You must align on whether your salary is considered "household money" or "personal money."
Question 16
"What is our budget for the wedding, and who is paying for it? Are we willing to go into debt to satisfy societal expectations?"
Why you must ask this: Desi weddings are notoriously exorbitant. If your partner is willing to drain your joint savings just to host a 1000-person Walima/Reception to impress distant relatives, your financial priorities are misaligned.

Module 3: Deconstructing Gender Roles

The diaspora exists in a liminal space. We are educated in modern, egalitarian systems, but raised in homes that often adhere to strict, traditional gender roles. You must uncover where your partner truly stands when the doors are closed.

Question 42
"If we both work 40 hours a week, exactly how will we divide the domestic mental load (cooking, cleaning, managing social calendars)?"
Why you must ask this: Notice the phrasing. Do not ask "Will you help around the house?" The word "help" implies the chore is inherently the woman's responsibility. You are looking for a partner who views domestic labor as a shared baseline, not a favor.
Question 43
"What were the gender dynamics like in your parents' marriage, and what specific aspects of their marriage do you NOT want to repeat in ours?"
Why you must ask this: We instinctively model the relationships we witnessed growing up. A partner who lacks the self-awareness to critique their parents' toxic relationship patterns is highly likely to repeat them.
